The condensing hive concept has taken off in the past year or so, and for good reason. Since I switched to condensing hives in my apiary, I've seen a 100% overwinter rate across my colonies. Leveraging condensing hives is a fantastic way to increase overwintering success rates and bolster the strength of overwintered colonies. 

## Intro to condensing hives

Instead of explaining all of the benefits of condensing hives here, I recommend watching [this excellent video from Peggy DeSanto](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tVSzRmnNcJ8) if you're interested in learning why condensing hives work.

The basic idea behind a condensing hive is that it closely mimics a wild colony's natural thermal characteristics. Langstroth hives are very unnatural in that they have minimal top insulation. This lack of insulation allows a colony's heat (and energy) to rapidly escape the hive in the winter. Even worse, when upper ventilation holes are added to a hive, we further increase the rate of heat loss and also prevent the colony from retaining precious water.

Condensing hives offer a solution to these problems - if we heavily insulate the top of the hive, and insulate the sides to a lesser extent, we can trap the colony's heat thereby reducing the amount of energy they need to expend to overwinter. The lack of upper ventilation holes prevents heat and water from leaving the hive. With insulation at the top of the hive, condensation collects around the sides of the inner cover (around the less-insulated side walls), not directly over the cluster, so there isn't any concern of cold water dripping on bees.

tl;dr, condensing hives have:

-  Extreme insulation at the top of the hive to retain heat and reduce energy consumption, with moderate insulation around the sides.
- No upper ventilation holes. Water is collected around the sides of the inner cover for re-use within the colony, not expelled from the hive.

## How can I make a condensing hive?

The good news is that there's nothing proprietary about a condensing hive. The concepts are simple, and there are multiple ways you can go about converting a ventilating hive to a condensing hive.

### Hive Huggers

 You could buy a[ Hive Hugger ](https://hivehugger.com/) kit for $100 per hive and call it a day, but there are draw backs that made me weary of this.

- The Hive Hugger system is proprietary and not easily repairable. I couldn't find any non-commercial sources for the high-density insulation used in the system.
- The side pieces need to be attached to the hive externally, making it difficult to manipulate wrapped hives for inspection.
- The kits would not fit my Lyson polystyrene hives, which had begun to replace my woodware at the time. 

These drawbacks (and the price of the system) were enough to convince me to find an alternative solution that I could use with my existing Lyson equipment.

### Lyson polystyrene hives and XPS boards

The solution that I ultimately landed on involves using Lyson's polystyrene hives from BetterBee as a base, and layering XPS boards within the hive for additional insulation. The Lyson hive components provide a somewhat adequate R-value of 6 without any modification, but by adding additional XPS boards within the hive, I was able to increase the R-value to **62** at the top of the hive.

Below, I've provided instructions on how you can replicate this using hive components that you can order online and insulation material that you can find at any hardware store. For now this is a text-only guide, I'll add pictures later.

> [!info]
> The instructions and bill of materials below are for a 10-frame deep hive configuration.

#### Bill of materials

- [1x Lyson polystyrene outer cover](https://www.betterbee.com/hives-and-components/lyson-w11010.asp)
- [1x Lyson inner cover](https://www.betterbee.com/bee-hives-and-components/lyson-w11970.asp)
- [2x Lyson deep hive body (one for the colony, one to fill with XPS boards)](https://www.betterbee.com/bee-hives-and-components/lyson-w11990-hive-body.asp)
- [1x Lyson bottom board](https://www.betterbee.com/hives-and-components/lyson-w11940.asp)
- [1x 2" R-10 XPS board](https://www.homedepot.com/p/Owens-Corning-FOAMULAR-NGX-F-250-2-in-x-4-ft-x-8-ft-SSE-R-10-XPS-Rigid-Foam-Board-Insulation-52DDNGX/315197962)
- [1x 1" R-5 XPS board](https://www.homedepot.com/p/Owens-Corning-FOAMULAR-NGX-F-150-1-in-x-4-ft-x-8-ft-SSE-R-5-XPS-Rigid-Foam-Board-Insulation-20WENGX/315197840)
- Some strong tape ([like strapping tape](https://www.lowes.com/pd/Scotch-1-1-97-in-Actual-Packing-Tape/1002255174))

A single 1" and 2" XPS board can provide enough materials for 3 condensing hives.

#### Construction

To start with, fully construct the Lyson equipment and paint all exterior surfaces with two coats of an exterior latex paint. I use really cheap stuff like [this](The Lyson hives themselves offer an adequate R-value of 6 without any modification,), but you can probably just use whatever's on hand.

The deeps go together with 12 long screws each. You'll also want to attach the provided latch kit, which we'll use to keep the hive components nice and snug. I recommend a battery-powered drill to make short work of assembling everything.

Go ahead and plug all of the holes in the outer cover with the provided [polystyrene plugs](https://www.betterbee.com/hives-and-components/lyheplugsolid-lyson-foam-plug-trapezoid.asp).

Next, it's time to cut the XPS boards. Make the following cuts from the 2" board using a Sawzall or other cutting tool:

- (1) 18 1/8" x 14 x 1/2" (**outer cover**)
- (3) 18 1/4" x 14 1/2" (**deep**)

And make the following cuts from the 1" board:

- (1) 18" x 14 1/2" (**deep, on frame rest**)
- (1) 18 1/4" x 14 1/2" (**deep**)

Now, assemble the hive:

1. Place the first deep on the bottom board and fill it with frames.
2. Place the inner cover on the first deep. Cover all of the circular ventilation holes with the provided polystyrene pucks.
3. Insert the (3) 2" XPS cuts labelled **deep** into the second deep, followed by the (1) 1" cut labelled **deep**. They should be inserted through the bottom of the box and pushed up all the way to the bottom of the frame rests. They should fit quite snugly, and may require some sanding to get all of them seated.
4. Install the second deep on top of the inner cover and place the other 1" cut of XPS labeled **deep, on frame rest** on top of the frame rest.
5. Place four strips of tape over the edges of the final 2" cut labeled **outer cover** - you'll use these as handles to pull the XPS out of the cover if needed.
6. Finally, place the 2" XPS cut into the outer cover. Place the outer cover on top of the hive and secure everything with the provided latches.

Done! With this build, we have achieved hive top insulation values of:

- Outer cover = R-16
- "Insulation deep" = R-40
- Inner cover = R-6

The total top insulation value with respect to the deep containing the colony is around R-62, with the sides at around R-6. Perfect for a condensing hive.

## How to manage insulated equipment throughout the year

Before each winter, I simply remove each hive's existing outer cover and add the insulation deep and the insulated outer cover above their inner covers. This instantly converts them to condensing hives, and I leave them in that configuration until warmer temperatures stabilize in the spring.

There's really no harm in keeping them in a condensing configuration for as long as possible. I only look to remove the insulation deep when the colony starts to show signs of outgrowing their existing deep(s) - then, I remove the insulation deep and replace with a regular frame-filled deep. I remove the XPS board from the outer cover at the same time - this is where the tape I mentioned comes in handy to pull the board out.

You can either store the insulation deeps as-is and reinstall them in the fall, or remove the boards from them and use them as regular deeps throughout the season. Same with the outer covers.

> [!warning]
> Remember to put your inner cover between the top-most box of your colony and the insulation deep - you don't want bees in your insulation box. If you forget this step, you'll probably end up with some wacky burr comb or chewed insulation. 

## Cautions

A few notes about what you can expect with this configuration:

1. If properly treated for mites and of appropriate size going into the winter, colonies overwintered in a condensing hive are generally much larger than colonies overwintered in a ventilating hive. Brood rearing will begin earlier because the colony is able to retain more heat with less energy, making it easier to stabilize at brood-rearing temperature. This can result in earlier swarming if you aren't paying attention.
2. Ants *will* find a way into your insulation box and ruin your XPS boards. Be proactive about this by deterring ants or placing your hives on ant-resistant stands.
3. The Lyson inner covers are rather fragile. I've broken several of them when prying up to separate them from the frames below. I haven't really found a good way around this - it seems like if your bees like to propolize their hive components together, sooner or later you'll break your inner cover. You can prolong their useful lives by prying slowly and methodically around each corner instead of prying hastily at one point.
